# Optiver Software Engineering Interview Overview

- **Research date:** 2026-07-24
- **Confidence:** Medium
- **Basis:** Public Optiver materials, job descriptions, and candidate reports. Specific sequencing can vary by office, seniority, and role.

## Executive summary

For software engineering candidates, Optiver's process is usually compact, selective, and deliberately multi-dimensional. A **typical end-to-end timeline is 2-4 weeks**, though it can move faster for priority hires or slower if interviewers are distributed across offices. The common arc is:

1. **Recruiter screen**
2. **Technical assessment / online assessment**
3. **One or more live video interviews** covering coding, systems, and behavior
4. **Final round** with multiple back-to-back interviews and deeper culture/role fit evaluation

The core pattern is consistent even when the exact labels differ: Optiver wants evidence that you can perform in a fast feedback, high-accountability engineering environment where decisions affect trading outcomes, reliability, and research velocity.

## Process at a glance

| Stage | Typical length | What it assesses | What strong candidates do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recruiter screen | 20-30 min | Motivation, communication, basic fit, logistics | Explain background crisply and show specific interest in Optiver |
| Technical assessment | 90 min to several hours, depending on role | Coding fluency, CS fundamentals, problem solving under time pressure, sometimes logic/reaction games | Stay calm, read carefully, write clean code, manage time |
| Video interviews | 45-60 min each | Live coding, debugging, system design, behavioral depth | Clarify requirements, reason aloud, connect choices to tradeoffs |
| Final round | Half day or several interviews | Seniority calibration, values alignment, ownership, breadth, cross-functional fit | Demonstrate judgment, transparency, and business-aware engineering |

## Stage 1: Recruiter screen

The recruiter screen is usually short, but it is not trivial. Recruiters are checking whether the rest of the process is worth scheduling.

### What this stage usually assesses

- Can you explain your career story clearly without wandering?
- Do you understand what Optiver is and why it differs from a generic big-tech role?
- Are your level, compensation expectations, location constraints, and timeline roughly aligned?
- Do you sound like someone who can operate in a direct, high-performance culture?

### What to expect

Typical questions include:

- "Walk me through your background."
- "Why Optiver?"
- "Why trading systems / market infrastructure / low-latency engineering?"
- "What kind of problems are you strongest at?"
- "What are you looking for in your next role?"

For senior candidates, recruiters also probe:

- Scope of ownership
- Leadership without authority
- Team size and mentoring exposure
- Cross-team influence
- Comfort with a fast, in-person, high-accountability environment

## Stage 2: Technical assessment

Public reports suggest that Optiver often uses an assessment that is broader than a standard algorithm screen. The exact structure varies, but candidates commonly report a mix of practical coding, computer science fundamentals, and occasionally game-like reasoning tasks.

### What this stage usually assesses

- Clean coding under time pressure
- Practical object-oriented or systems reasoning, not just memorized patterns
- Comfort with ambiguity in prompts
- Ability to make progress without perfect information
- Stamina and focus over a relatively demanding assessment

### Preparation implications

Do not prepare for Optiver as if it is only a LeetCode filter. You still need solid data structures and algorithms, but many candidates report being surprised by:

- Longer, more realistic prompts
- OOP or implementation-heavy tasks
- Questions where clarification and careful reading matter as much as raw speed
- CS fundamentals outside pure coding, such as concurrency, memory, networking, or complexity reasoning

## Stage 3: Video interviews

After the assessment, candidates typically move into live interviews over video. Depending on role and seniority, these may include 2-4 interviews across technical and behavioral dimensions.

### Common live interview topics

#### Coding / debugging

Interviewers want more than a working answer. They look for:

- Requirement clarification
- Sensible edge cases
- Readable structure
- Runtime and memory awareness
- Willingness to test assumptions aloud

#### System design

For senior engineers, this is often where level is decided. Questions may be framed around services, pipelines, data flow, reliability, observability, or latency-sensitive systems.

Strong answers usually include:

- Explicit requirements and constraints
- Clear tradeoff discussion
- Failure modes and mitigation
- Operational concerns: deployment, rollback, monitoring, alerting
- Awareness of organizational boundaries, not just box-and-arrow diagrams

#### Behavioral / project deep dive

Optiver tends to use behavioral interviews to validate how you actually work, especially under pressure. They are looking for evidence of ownership, direct communication, curiosity, and collaboration.

## Stage 4: Final round

The final round often compresses several conversations into one block. Titles vary by office, but the final stage usually answers one question: **Would we trust this person with meaningful engineering responsibility in a high-stakes environment?**

### What final rounds often assess

- Depth in one or two technical areas
- Breadth across systems, reliability, and collaboration
- How you communicate under sustained scrutiny
- Whether your values fit an open, direct culture
- Whether your examples sound genuinely senior rather than merely well rehearsed

For some teams, final rounds may include additional discussions with engineering leadership or partner functions. For senior engineers, expect more probing on incident ownership, technical direction, prioritization, and influence.

## Timeline expectations

A **2-4 week process** is a reasonable default expectation.

### Common timing pattern

| Week | Likely activity |
| --- | --- |
| Week 1 | Recruiter outreach/screen and scheduling |
| Week 1-2 | Online assessment |
| Week 2-3 | Live technical and behavioral interviews |
| Week 3-4 | Final round, debrief, decision |

### What can make it faster or slower

Faster:

- Role is urgent
- Interviewers are in one office/time zone
- Candidate has strong availability

Slower:

- Cross-office scheduling
- Senior-level calibration discussions
- Team matching after technical pass
- Holiday periods or interview backlog

## Optiver values likely reflected in interviews

Public-facing materials and role descriptions frequently emphasize **collaboration, transparency, innovation, and curiosity**. Even when interviewers do not state these as a checklist, the process tends to test them.

| Value | How it shows up in interviews |
| --- | --- |
| Collaboration | Cross-team stories, mentoring, handling disagreement productively |
| Transparency | Clear status updates, admitting mistakes, surfacing risk early |
| Innovation | Improving tooling, challenging defaults, making systems better |
| Curiosity | Asking sharp questions, learning fast, understanding domain context |

## What senior engineers demonstrate at Optiver

A senior candidate is usually expected to show more than personal coding strength.

### Signals that read as senior

- **System-level thinking:** You understand second-order effects, dependencies, and operational risk.
- **Proactive communication:** You do not wait until things fail to surface risk or misalignment.
- **Standards setting:** You improve how the team works, not just what you individually ship.
- **Business awareness:** You connect engineering decisions to latency, reliability, developer velocity, or trading outcomes.
- **Calm ownership:** In incidents or ambiguity, you make the situation clearer for everyone else.

### Signals that read as too junior for the level

- Describing only assigned tasks rather than choices you shaped
- Treating system design as diagrams without operations
- Talking about incidents as someone else's problem
- Framing collaboration as "I convinced them" instead of "we aligned on tradeoffs"

## Preparation priorities

If you are optimizing limited prep time, prioritize in this order:

1. **Story preparation for behavioral depth**  
   Have concise, senior-level stories on incidents, tradeoffs, disagreement, mentoring, and ambiguity.
2. **Live coding in a practical style**  
   Practice writing readable code while narrating assumptions and tests.
3. **System design with operational realism**  
   Include monitoring, rollback, failure handling, and stakeholder communication.
4. **Role motivation**  
   Be able to explain why Optiver specifically, not just "finance is interesting."
5. **Domain curiosity**  
   You do not need to pretend to be a trader, but you should sound energized by high-performance, high-feedback systems.

## Final guidance

Treat the process as an evaluation of how you would operate inside a demanding engineering culture, not as a sequence of isolated tests. The strongest candidates combine technical rigor with direct communication, good judgment, and visible curiosity. Because public evidence comes from a mix of job descriptions and candidate reports, keep a **medium-confidence** stance on exact sequencing. The broad message is more stable than the exact order: Optiver is looking for engineers who can think clearly, collaborate openly, and own outcomes in a fast-moving environment.
