# Optiver Behavioral Question Themes

## How to use this guide

These are the question themes most commonly implied by Optiver job descriptions, public interview advice, and candidate reports. The exact wording will vary, but the underlying tests are fairly stable. For each theme below, prepare **one or two strong stories** rather than memorizing dozens of answers.

## 1. Technical leadership and mentoring

### Example questions

1. "Tell me about a time you raised the bar for other engineers."
2. "How have you mentored someone who was struggling?"
3. "Describe a technical decision where people looked to you for guidance."
4. "How do you keep code review from becoming a rubber stamp?"
5. "What's an example of improving engineering standards on a team?"

### What this is really assessing

- Whether you create leverage beyond personal output
- Whether others trust your judgment
- Whether you can teach without ego
- Whether your leadership changes how the team operates after you leave the room

### What strong answers include

- a specific team weakness or gap,
- your diagnosis of the root problem,
- a concrete intervention,
- evidence that the improvement persisted.

## 2. Handling ambiguity and unclear requirements

### Example questions

1. "Tell me about a project where the requirements were not well defined."
2. "When have you had to make progress without all the data you wanted?"
3. "Describe a time priorities kept changing."
4. "How do you avoid analysis paralysis?"
5. "Tell me about a decision you made under uncertainty."

### What this is really assessing

- Whether you can reduce ambiguity instead of complaining about it
- Whether you know when to ask questions versus when to decide
- Whether you communicate assumptions visibly
- Whether you protect delivery without pretending certainty you do not have

### What strong answers include

- explicit assumptions,
- a decision framework,
- stakeholder alignment,
- a mechanism to revisit the decision as new information arrived.

## 3. Disagreements with colleagues or managers

### Example questions

1. "Tell me about a time you strongly disagreed with a teammate."
2. "Describe a technical disagreement with your manager."
3. "When did you have to push back on a plan?"
4. "How have you handled conflict between speed and quality?"
5. "Tell me about a time you changed your mind after debate."

### What this is really assessing

- Whether you can be direct without being destructive
- Whether you use evidence instead of status games
- Whether you can commit once a decision is made
- Whether you preserve trust while arguing hard on the merits

### What strong answers include

- the substantive disagreement,
- the criteria used to evaluate options,
- how the conversation stayed respectful,
- what happened after the decision.

## 4. Production incidents and ownership

### Example questions

1. "Tell me about the worst incident you handled."
2. "Describe a time you discovered an issue before it became an outage."
3. "How have you handled a rollback or degraded launch?"
4. "Tell me about a mistake that impacted production."
5. "How do you run a useful postmortem?"

### What this is really assessing

- Composure under pressure
- Clear communication during uncertainty
- Root-cause thinking instead of surface-level patching
- Sense of responsibility for prevention, not just resolution

### What strong answers include

- timeline clarity,
- who was informed and when,
- incident command behavior,
- corrective and preventive actions,
- lessons that changed future practice.

## 5. System design decisions and tradeoffs

### Example questions

1. "Tell me about a major architecture decision you influenced."
2. "Describe a time you chose simplicity over maximum performance."
3. "When did you deliberately accept technical debt?"
4. "How have you balanced reliability against feature delivery?"
5. "Tell me about a design you regret and what you learned."

### What this is really assessing

- Whether you can reason in tradeoffs rather than absolutes
- Whether you see operational and organizational consequences
- Whether your decisions reflect business impact, not engineering aesthetics alone

### What strong answers include

- constraints,
- alternatives considered,
- decision criteria,
- measured impact,
- a sober reflection on what was gained and sacrificed.

## 6. Collaboration across teams

### Example questions

1. "Tell me about a project that required multiple teams to align."
2. "How have you handled dependency risk from another team?"
3. "Describe a time you had to translate between technical and non-technical groups."
4. "When did you improve collaboration at an interface between teams?"
5. "Tell me about a partnership that was initially difficult."

### What this is really assessing

- Whether you can build momentum without formal authority
- Whether you understand incentives on both sides of an interface
- Whether you reduce coordination overhead rather than adding to it

### What strong answers include

- stakeholder mapping,
- communication rhythms,
- decision records,
- escalation only when needed,
- durable interface improvements.

## 7. Learning from failure

### Example questions

1. "Tell me about a failure that changed how you work."
2. "Describe a project that did not meet expectations."
3. "When did you misjudge a technical risk?"
4. "Tell me about feedback that was hard to hear."
5. "What's a mistake you would not repeat today?"

### What this is really assessing

- Whether you are self-aware and coachable
- Whether you extract transferable lessons
- Whether you can discuss failure without becoming evasive or melodramatic

### What strong answers include

- honest ownership,
- proportionate responsibility,
- practical learning,
- evidence of changed behavior later.

## 8. Motivation and domain curiosity

### Example questions

1. "Why Optiver rather than a generic infrastructure role?"
2. "What about trading systems interests you?"
3. "How do you learn a new technical domain quickly?"
4. "Tell me about a time curiosity led you to improve something important."
5. "What technical area are you currently exploring and why?"

### What this is really assessing

- Whether your interest is real, not borrowed from compensation headlines
- Whether you enjoy learning in high-feedback environments
- Whether curiosity produces better engineering outcomes for you

### What strong answers include

- specific reasons,
- informed enthusiasm,
- examples of domain learning,
- connection between technical work and business consequences.

## How to prepare efficiently

For each theme, write a one-page prep note with:

- a headline sentence,
- context and stakes,
- your role,
- the hardest decision,
- the measurable result,
- what you learned,
- two other questions the same story can answer.

If you do that well, you can handle most Optiver behavioral questions without sounding scripted.
