# Questions to Ask Interviewers at Optiver

## Why your questions matter

At Optiver, the questions you ask are part of the evaluation. Good questions signal curiosity, judgment, and seriousness about the work. Weak questions can make you sound generic, passive, or overly focused on perks before impact.

A good Optiver question usually does at least one of these things:

- explores how engineering decisions connect to trading outcomes,
- reveals how teams communicate and make decisions,
- probes where the hardest technical problems really are,
- clarifies what success looks like at senior level.

## 1. Team structure and engineering culture

### Strong questions

- "How is this team structured around a trading or platform workflow, and where do the main engineering interfaces sit?"
- "What does direct feedback look like here when people disagree on a technical approach?"
- "What differentiates engineers who are solid from engineers who become high-trust people in the organization?"
- "How much autonomy does the team have in setting technical direction versus reacting to immediate business needs?"

### Why these work

They show that you care about decision quality, not just reporting lines. They also invite the interviewer to describe real operating norms.

## 2. Technical stack and architecture decisions

### Strong questions

- "What are the most important architectural constraints the team works within today: latency, determinism, reliability, developer velocity, or something else?"
- "Where has the team deliberately chosen simplicity over theoretical performance, or the reverse?"
- "What parts of the system generate the most debate because the tradeoffs are genuinely hard?"
- "How do you decide when to build internal tooling versus adopt external platforms?"

### Why these work

These questions signal that you think in tradeoffs. They are especially good for senior candidates because they invite a technical conversation instead of a brochure answer.

## 3. Career growth and mentorship

### Strong questions

- "What does senior growth look like here beyond title progression?"
- "How do strong engineers expand scope at Optiver without becoming disconnected from the technical work?"
- "What mentoring expectations do senior engineers usually carry?"
- "How do new hires learn enough domain context to make good decisions quickly?"

### Why these work

They show that you are thinking about long-term contribution, leverage, and learning speed.

## 4. Product and trading strategy from a systems perspective

### Strong questions

- "Without getting into proprietary strategy details, what system characteristics matter most to this team's edge?"
- "Where does engineering most directly influence research velocity or trading performance?"
- "What kinds of feedback loops exist between engineers, traders, and researchers when a system is underperforming?"
- "How do market structure changes tend to affect technical priorities?"

### Why these work

They communicate domain curiosity without pretending to know confidential details. That balance is valuable.

## 5. Work-life balance and on-call

### Strong questions

- "What does operational ownership look like in practice for this team?"
- "How often do engineers deal with time-sensitive incidents outside standard hours?"
- "What has the team done recently to make on-call or incident response more sustainable?"
- "How do you distinguish between healthy urgency and chronic firefighting?"

### Why these work

They are practical and mature. They frame work-life questions around operating model and sustainability rather than avoidance of effort.

## 6. Questions that show intellectual curiosity

### Strong questions

- "What technical problem seems straightforward from the outside but is much harder in your environment?"
- "If you could remove one recurring source of engineering friction in the next year, what would it be?"
- "What assumptions about high-performance systems do new hires often get wrong?"
- "What is a recent technical decision the team is proud of, and why?"

### Why these work

They invite stories, not scripts. Interviewers usually enjoy answering them because they reveal real substance.

## Good sequencing strategy

If you only have time for 2-3 questions, ask in this order:

1. **What matters most technically on this team right now?**
2. **How does the team make hard tradeoffs or disagreements visible?**
3. **What distinguishes a truly strong senior engineer here?**

That sequence shows technical interest, culture fit awareness, and ambition without sounding performative.

## Questions to avoid

### Avoid early questions that are mostly generic

- "What does the company do?"
- "What tech stack do you use?" with no follow-up on why
- "How many vacation days are there?" as your first question
- "How quickly can I get promoted?"

### Avoid questions that imply weak ownership

- "How often will someone check my work?"
- "How much of the job is meetings versus real work?"
- "Would I have to deal with incidents?"

### Avoid questions that push for sensitive information

- Detailed proprietary trading strategy questions
- Requests for confidential performance metrics
- Questions that sound like fishing for market edge details unrelated to your role

## How to read interviewer reactions

### Positive signals

- The interviewer answers at length and adds concrete examples.
- They engage with your follow-up rather than reverting to boilerplate.
- They start volunteering nuance, tensions, or recent changes.

### Neutral signals

- The interviewer answers politely but briefly.
- The question may have been too broad, or you may need a sharper follow-up.

### Warning signs

- They repeatedly give vague answers to straightforward culture or ownership questions.
- Different interviewers describe success in contradictory ways.
- On-call, mentoring, or decision-making questions generate visible defensiveness.

One ambiguous answer is not decisive. A pattern of evasive answers is more meaningful.

## Final advice

The best questions at Optiver are specific, systems-minded, and curiosity-driven. They should help you evaluate the role while simultaneously showing how you think. If your questions reveal that you care about technical tradeoffs, transparent culture, and the connection between engineering and outcomes, you will leave a stronger impression than a candidate who only asks about process or perks.
