# Optiver Behavioral Interview Simulation (60 Minutes, Deeper Version)

**Target candidate:** Senior C++ engineer at Deutsche Bank, 3 years of experience  
**Purpose:** Simulate a deeper behavioral round that probes for senior engineering signal, not just basic STAR competence.  
**Focus areas:** leadership, technical ownership, collaboration, conflict, growth, and operating style in a high-performance environment.

---

## What “senior engineering signal” looks like here

Even with only three years of experience, you can present as senior if you consistently demonstrate:

- **scope judgment:** you can tell what matters and why;
- **ownership:** you move work forward without waiting for perfect clarity;
- **technical credibility:** you understand the details well enough to make tradeoffs;
- **communication:** you reduce confusion, especially under pressure;
- **learning velocity:** you absorb feedback and improve quickly;
- **business awareness:** you know the engineering goal is not elegance alone, but useful outcomes.

This interview will test whether you can combine all six.

## Full interviewer opening script

> Thanks for joining. In this conversation I’m less interested in polished leadership language and more interested in how you actually behave in real situations. I’ll ask for details, I may challenge assumptions, and I may ask what you would do differently. The goal is to understand your judgment, not catch you out. Let’s start simple: give me a concise overview of the type of engineer you are.

## Question 1 — Leadership without authority

**Interviewer prompt**

> Tell me about a time you led something important without being the formal owner or manager.

**Harder follow-up probes**

- What made other people follow your lead?
- Tell me more about the moment you realized passive participation would not be enough.
- What resistance did you face?
- How did you avoid overstepping?

**Evaluation criteria**

- Informal leadership
- Influence through clarity and competence
- Boundary awareness
- Ability to create momentum

**Strong answer example**

> A good example was a cross-team issue where the nominal ownership sat elsewhere, but the problem kept affecting a system my team relied on. I wasn’t the manager or official owner, but I recognized that waiting for perfect alignment would keep the issue unresolved. I stepped in by structuring the problem, gathering concrete evidence, and making the decision points explicit so the right people could move faster. I was careful not to act as if I owned everyone’s roadmap; my role was to reduce ambiguity and make action easier. That turned out to be enough to create momentum.

## Question 2 — Technical ownership

**Interviewer prompt**

> Tell me about a system or component you felt truly responsible for.

**Harder follow-up probes**

- If it broke at 2 a.m., what would you know that others would not?
- What tradeoffs did you knowingly accept in the design?
- Tell me more about the ugliest part of that system.
- What would you do differently if you could redesign it now?

**Evaluation criteria**

- Depth of understanding
- Pride without defensiveness
- Comfort discussing constraints and flaws
- Ownership beyond implementation

**Strong answer example**

> The best example for me is a component where I became the person others came to when the behavior was unclear, performance regressed, or rollout risk needed to be assessed. What made it feel like true ownership was not that I wrote every line; it was that I understood the operational behavior, failure modes, and tradeoffs well enough to make good decisions under uncertainty. I could explain what we optimized for, where the design was brittle, and what evidence I would want before changing it.

**Transition line**

## Question 3 — Conflict with a strong stakeholder

**Interviewer prompt**

> Tell me about a conflict with a stakeholder whose goals were not naturally aligned with yours.

**Harder follow-up probes**

- Why did they see the world differently?
- What did you initially misunderstand about their perspective?
- Tell me more about how the conversation changed from tension to progress.
- Did you win the argument, or did you find a better framing?

**Evaluation criteria**

- Empathy without losing standards
- Ability to find the real disagreement
- Negotiation and reframing skill

**Strong answer example**

> In one case, the conflict looked technical on the surface, but the deeper difference was incentive structure. I was focused on system risk and long-term maintainability, while the stakeholder was incentivized by immediate delivery pressure. Once I understood that, the conversation got much more productive. Instead of arguing from “good engineering,” I reframed the discussion in terms of controllable delivery risk, observability, and rollback safety. That gave us a shared decision framework rather than a personality clash.

## Question 4 — Failure and growth

**Interviewer prompt**

> Tell me about a time you got something wrong in a way that mattered.

**Harder follow-up probes**

- What exactly was your mistake?
- Tell me more about what you missed at the time.
- How did you repair trust afterward?
- What evidence shows you changed, instead of just promising to change?

**Evaluation criteria**

- Honesty
- Accountability
- Behavioral adaptation
- Ability to recover credibility

**Strong answer example**

> A meaningful example for me was a case where I moved too quickly from diagnosis to solution because I felt pressure to show momentum. The implementation itself was not reckless, but the framing was incomplete: I had not aligned the team sufficiently on the assumptions and decision criteria. That created avoidable friction later. The important part was not just admitting that; it was changing my operating model. Since then, I’ve become much more deliberate about writing down assumptions, surfacing risks earlier, and validating the problem before optimizing the solution.

## Question 5 — Collaboration under pace

**Interviewer prompt**

> Describe a time you had to collaborate very closely with people who processed information differently from you.

**Harder follow-up probes**

- Who needed what from you that did not come naturally?
- How did you adapt your communication style?
- Tell me more about one moment where misalignment was expensive.
- How do you keep speed without creating confusion?

**Evaluation criteria**

- Communication range
- Adaptability
- Respect for different working styles
- Ability to preserve speed and clarity together

**Strong answer example**

> I’ve learned that technical alignment often breaks down not because people are incapable, but because they are optimizing for different things and absorb information differently. In one project, some stakeholders wanted a concise decision, while others needed the reasoning trail to trust the decision. I adapted by separating the executive summary from the supporting detail, which reduced repeated debates. That sounds simple, but it materially improved execution speed because people stopped talking past each other.

## Question 6 — Raising the bar

**Interviewer prompt**

> Tell me about a time you raised the standard on your team.

**Harder follow-up probes**

- What was the old standard, and why was it tolerated?
- Tell me more about how you convinced people the bar should move.
- Was there any downside to pushing higher?
- How did you make the improvement stick?

**Evaluation criteria**

- Initiative beyond assigned tasks
- Good taste in what standards matter
- Change management
- Sustainability of improvement

**Strong answer example**

> The most credible form of raising the bar is changing a repeated pattern, not winning one isolated argument. In one area, I noticed we were repeatedly paying the same operational tax because the system was hard to debug under stress. Rather than treating each incident as isolated, I pushed for a better standard around instrumentation and runbook quality. The key was making the cost visible. Once people saw how much time and risk the current state was creating, raising the bar stopped sounding abstract.

## Question 7 — Why this step now?

**Interviewer prompt**

> Why is now the right time for you to move into a firm like Optiver, and what do you expect to find difficult at first?

**Harder follow-up probes**

- What do you think people from banking often underestimate about this transition?
- Tell me more about how you plan to close your biggest gap.
- Why should we believe you’ll ramp quickly enough?
- What would success in your first year look like?

**Evaluation criteria**

- Motivation grounded in reality
- Humility about the gap
- Confidence supported by preparation
- Clear first-year success model

**Strong answer example**

> I think now is the right time because I’ve built enough production depth to be genuinely useful, but I still have a steep appetite for learning and want a more demanding environment. I’m not assuming the transition is trivial. I expect the hardest parts to be ramping on the pace of decision-making, the tighter performance constraints, and the domain context around market structure. What gives me confidence is that the underlying skill set I’ve been building—reasoning about production systems, diagnosing performance issues, making tradeoffs under pressure, and learning quickly from stronger engineers—maps well to the challenge.

## Self-assessment rubric

| Dimension | Weak | Competent | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leadership | Needed title to lead | Sometimes drove alignment | Influenced through clarity and trust |
| Technical ownership | Knows tasks | Knows component basics | Understands behavior, tradeoffs, and failure modes |
| Conflict handling | Defensive or avoidant | Professional but generic | Reframes conflict into better decisions |
| Growth mindset | Says the right words | Has one feedback story | Demonstrates real behavioral change |
| Collaboration | Friendly but vague | Works well enough | Adapts style while preserving standards |
| Standards | Accepts team norms | Improves local practices | Raises durable team-wide quality bar |
| Fit for Optiver | Wants brand | Understands role somewhat | Motivated by pace, rigor, and engineering density |

### Scoring guidance

- **Mostly strong:** ready for live interview with only polishing needed
- **Mostly competent:** credible, but stories need sharper stakes and outcomes
- **Any weak dimension:** replace that story before using this with interviewers

## Final coaching note

For a deeper behavioral round, polished stories are not enough. The interviewer is listening for a repeatable operating system: **how you think, how you decide, how you influence, and how you improve**. That is the level you should practice toward.
