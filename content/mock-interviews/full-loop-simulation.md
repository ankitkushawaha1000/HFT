# Full Loop Simulation (4 Hours)

**Target candidate:** Senior C++ engineer at Deutsche Bank preparing for Optiver and peer HFT firms  
**Purpose:** Rehearse the full day as a performance event: pacing, recovery between rounds, answer consistency, and debrief discipline.

---

## Full loop schedule

| Time | Activity | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| 00:00-00:30 | Interview 1 — Recruiter screen | Establish narrative, motivation, logistics fit |
| 00:30-00:40 | Break 1 | Reset tone and energy |
| 00:40-01:40 | Interview 2 — Behavioral with hiring manager | Demonstrate ownership, judgment, collaboration |
| 01:40-01:50 | Break 2 | Recover mentally and annotate signals |
| 01:50-02:50 | Interview 3 — Senior C++ technical | Show depth, precision, and calm under probing |
| 02:50-03:00 | Break 3 | Physically reset before design round |

## Interview 1 — Recruiter screen (30 minutes)

### What success looks like

- You sound crisp, energized, and deliberate.
- Your move from banking to HFT feels logical.
- You present as high-upside, coachable, and serious.
- You leave the recruiter with an easy “advance” recommendation.

### Key questions to rehearse

- Walk me through your background.
- Why are you exploring now?
- Why HFT and why our firm?
- What project best represents your fit?
- What questions do you have for me?

### Specific success criteria

- Background answer under 2 minutes.
- One technical ownership story ready.
- One incident story ready.
- No negativity about current employer.

---

## Break 1 (10 minutes)

### What to do

- Stand up and leave the desk.
- Drink water.
- Write three bullets only:
  1. What story seemed strongest?
  2. What answer ran too long?
  3. What detail do I need to carry consistently into later rounds?

### What not to do

- Do not reread every note.
- Do not start critiquing your whole career narrative.
- Do not burn mental energy on imagined mistakes.

---

## Interview 2 — Behavioral with hiring manager (60 minutes)

### What success looks like

- Stories show judgment, not just effort.
- You sound direct but not rigid.
- You demonstrate senior signal despite three years of experience.
- You connect technical decisions to real outcomes.

### Themes to cover

- Ownership under ambiguity
- Conflict with strong stakeholders
- Feedback and growth
- Working under pressure
- Why this environment fits you

### Build on the recruiter screen

Do not restart from zero. Reuse your strongest themes, but at greater depth.

**Example:**
- Recruiter round: “I’ve taken on more performance-sensitive production work.”
- Behavioral round: tell the specific story that proves it.

### Hiring manager decision factors

- Would this person take responsibility when stakes rise?
- Can this person work well with smart, opinionated peers?
- Will this person ramp fast enough from banking into trading?

---

## Break 2 (10 minutes)

### Fast debrief template

Write one line for each:

- **Ownership signal I landed:** `CANDIDATE_TODO`
- **Story that still needs sharper numbers:** `CANDIDATE_TODO`
- **Behavioral trait I want to reinforce in technical rounds:** calm / precise / structured / curious

### Reset instruction

Your next round is not “more of the same.” Shift from narrative mode to precision mode.

---

## Interview 3 — Technical C++ (60 minutes)

### What success looks like

- You reason from first principles.
- You use correct terminology without showing off.
- You talk aloud clearly during uncertainty.
- You keep the coding challenge simple and correct.

### Core areas to simulate

- Warm-up C++ fundamentals
- Memory management and custom allocator discussion
- Concurrency and atomics reasoning
- Template constraints or metaprogramming design
- Lock-free ring buffer coding task

### How to build on earlier rounds

Use your behavioral content selectively:

- If asked why you care about a particular performance issue, anchor it in a real production example.
- If asked about tradeoffs, show the same judgment style you described behaviorally.

### Failure modes in this round

- Talking in slogans: “avoid heap allocations,” “use atomics,” “make it cache-friendly.”
- Going silent while coding.
- Over-engineering the ring buffer instead of finishing a correct simple version.

---

## Break 3 (10 minutes)

### Physical reset checklist

- Walk for 2-3 minutes.
- Relax shoulders and jaw.
- Avoid screens for part of the break.
- Write down only two notes:
  - one technical point you explained well;
  - one design concept you must remember to articulate in the next round.

### Mental reset

System design rewards structure more than raw language trivia. Slow down slightly at the start.

---

## Interview 4 — System design (60 minutes)

### What success looks like

- You clarify before you design.
- You decompose the problem into components cleanly.
- You treat sequencing, correctness, and recovery as first-class concerns.
- You explain tradeoffs rather than rushing to “the answer.”

### Focus problem

Use `content/mock-interviews/system-design-60min.md` and design a market data feed handler for multiple exchanges.

### Signals the interviewer wants

- Operational maturity
- Latency awareness
- Ability to discuss degraded modes and recovery
- Communication that a trading-facing team could trust

### How to build on earlier rounds

- Reuse your narrative of being strong in production systems.
- Reinforce consistency: you are the engineer who brings clarity under pressure.

## Energy and pacing advice for the full day

### 1. Start slightly below maximum intensity

If you come in at 100% intensity from minute one, you will often sound rushed by round three. Aim for composed energy, not performative excitement.

### 2. Keep a stable answer framework

Across all rounds, use the same internal structure:

- context;
- problem;
- your role;
- tradeoff or decision;
- outcome;
- reflection.

That consistency reduces cognitive load.

### 3. Do not overcorrect between rounds

A mediocre answer in one round should not cause you to become robotic in the next. Reset, note it, move on.

### 4. Protect your voice and breathing

Technical and design rounds get worse when your breathing becomes shallow. Short pause, then answer.

### 5. Treat breaks as part of the interview

The candidate who resets well often outperforms the candidate who knows slightly more but degrades over time.

---

## How to build on earlier answers without sounding repetitive

### Reuse themes, not scripts

Your strongest loop-wide themes might be:

- performance-sensitive production systems;
- calm incident handling;
- structured thinking;
- direct but low-ego collaboration;
- desire for tighter engineering feedback loops.

Bring those themes forward, but with different evidence and depth each time.

### Escalate specificity by round

- **Recruiter:** broad narrative
- **Behavioral:** concrete stories
- **Technical:** detailed mechanisms and tradeoffs
- **Design:** architecture and operational reasoning

### Maintain factual consistency

If you say you owned a system in one round, be prepared to describe:

- what the system did;
- what scale mattered;
- what your actual responsibility was;
- what changed because of your work.

## End-of-day debrief structure (20-30 minutes)

### Part 1 — Immediate factual capture

Write down:

- every question you can remember;
- where you hesitated;
- what examples resonated;
- what follow-up probes were hardest.

### Part 2 — Signal assessment

Rate yourself from 1-5 on:

- narrative clarity;
- ownership;
- technical precision;
- system design structure;
- communication under pressure;
- consistency across rounds.

### Part 3 — Hiring-committee view

Ask:

- Would the recruiter advocate for me?
- Would the hiring manager trust me with ambiguous work?
- Would the technical interviewer believe I can operate in a performance-critical C++ environment?
- Would the design interviewer believe I can reason about correctness and recovery in trading systems?

### Part 4 — Action items

Create exactly three next-step actions. Example:

1. Replace weak conflict story with better stakeholder example.
2. Rehearse acquire/release explanation for SPSC queue.
3. Tighten system design opening to spend more time on recovery.

## Overall rubric and likely hiring decision factors

| Dimension | Weak hire signal | Strong hire signal |
|---|---|---|
| Motivation | Wants prestige or comp only | Wants the work, pace, and engineering density |
| Communication | Rambling or defensive | Clear, direct, calm |
| Ownership | Participated well | Drove outcomes under ambiguity |
| Technical depth | Knows syntax and patterns | Reasons precisely about performance and correctness |
| System design | Generic diagrams | Good decomposition and operational realism |
| Growth trajectory | Plateau risk | Steep learning curve and high upside |

### Likely “yes” decision pattern

A strong loop usually produces a summary like this:

> Strong communication, credible systems background, solid C++ fundamentals, good ownership for tenure, thoughtful motivation for HFT, and clear upside with manageable domain ramp.

### Likely “no” decision pattern

> Smart candidate, but too generic on behavioral answers, uneven on concurrency/performance details, and not yet convincing enough on why they will ramp fast in this environment.

---

## Final instruction for simulation day

Run the four interviews in order, with real timing and real breaks. Do not pause to perfect individual answers mid-session. The purpose is to practice **interview endurance, answer consistency, and decision-quality under fatigue**, because that is exactly what the real loop tests.
